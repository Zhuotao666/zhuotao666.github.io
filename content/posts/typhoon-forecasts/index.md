Weather and climate forecasting has spanned human history for thousands of years, with substantial improvements in accuracy driven by advances in meteorological science and numerical computational methods. As climate change has emerged as one of the central challenges of the 21st century, accompanied by increasingly frequent and intense weather-related natural disasters such as floods, typhoons, extreme temperatures, the importance of weather and climate forecasting has grown significantly. In low to mid latitude coastal regions, typhoons have posed recurring and severe risks to human lives and economic activities for thousands of years. The ability of scientific forecasting agencies to accurately predict cyclone paths and intensities, and the capacity of local governments to make timely decisions translating those predictions into public warnings, has become increasingly important for avoidance against typhoon damages in the populous and economically active coastal regions of Southern and Eastern China.

Despite the significant progress in forecasting technology, an important question remains insufficiently understood in social science literature: how effective are weather forecasts in generating economically meaningful adaptations? In particular, improvements in forecast accuracy do not automatically translate into improved welfare if forecasts are not properly communicated through delivery of local typhoon warnings. This paper examines the efficiency of typhoon forecasting systems in China by jointly analyzing forecast accuracy, government alert policies, and realized economic responses, focusing on documented typhoon events affecting China over 2021-2024.

We combine three novel datasets collected from multiple international and domestic sources. We assemble high-resolution data on realized typhoon tracks and wind fields, including wind radii (wind rings) at Level 7 or 34 knots intensity thresholds, based on post-event realized or calibrated best-track data. We collect forecast data from multiple meteorological agencies, including the China Meteorological Administration (CMA), the Hong Kong Observatory, the Japan Meteorological Agency, and the Joint Typhoon Warning Center (JTWC). These forecasts include predicted cyclone tracks, central pressure, and wind speeds, which we use to simulate forecast-implied wind exposure at fine geographic scales. Third, we obtain the detailed records of central and local government typhoon alerts in China, including the timing and intensity of warnings (e.g., blue, yellow, orange, red alerts) issued from national to district levels. Merging these datasets, we construct a district-day panel dataset linking forecasted, alerted, and realized typhoon exposures.

Our first set of results documents substantial economic impacts of typhoons and highlights the role of early warnings in mitigating these effects. Using a panel difference-in-differences regression design, we find that typhoon exposure under no local alerts leads to an average reduction in daily-district level nightlight intensity of approximately 4%, reflecting disruptions to local economic activity. The issuance of timely typhoon alerts at blue-level or higher, issued prior to typhoon exposures, by city or district governments, can completely attenuate these losses. To address potential endogeneity in local typhoon alert issuance, we employ instrumental variable regressions using the inverse of the distance between forecasted typhoon tracks (by CMA or other foreign agencies) and district centers as an instrument for local alerts. The IV estimates suggest that the mitigating effect of alerts may be substantially larger, recovering reductions in economic losses at almost 50% nightlight reduction. These findings provide the causal evidence that forecast-based public information generates efficient economic adaptation.

Second, we compare the accuracy of typhoon forecasts across different meteorological agencies. We find that while forecast errors exhibit broadly similar patterns across agencies, such as increased difficulty in predicting the routes of larger or more complex storms, there are notable differences in forecast performance across different dimensions. For example, the CMA tends to exhibit lower forecast errors in wind speed, while the Hong Kong Observatory performs relatively better in predicting storm track locations. These differences suggest that forecast systems may be complementary rather than strictly competing, and that integrating information across sources could potentially improve overall predictive performance.

Third, we examine the translation of forecasts into government alert decisions. By simulating optimal alert issuance based on forecasted typhoon locations, wind speeds and pressures based on China's central and local policy guidance on typhoon alert issuance, we find that simulated and actual alerts have a high overall correlation exceeding 92%, indicating that the alert system is broadly aligned with forecast information as instructed by regulations. However, we also identify some preliminary systematic deviations, including on average under-issuance and delayed issuance of alerts. These deviations may reflect underlying political economy considerations regarding the trade-offs with reduced local economic activities and public panicking with early alert issuance. To interpret these patterns, we are developing a simple theoretical model of local government decision on typhoon alert issuance, taking into account both losses from typhoon damage and short-term economic costs associated with precautionary shutdowns. In this framework, local officials may optimally delay or suppress alerts when the perceived economic costs of precautionary measures outweigh the expected benefits of risk reduction, especially under uncertainty about storm realization due to inaccurate forecasts. Consistent with this mechanism, we have also found preliminary evidence that larger gaps between simulated optimal alerts and actual alert issuance are associated with greater subsequent economic losses.

To summarize, our findings have highlighted that the efficiency of typhoon forecasting system in China depends not only on technological accuracy but also on the institutional processes that govern how forecasts are used for public information communications. In our ongoing work, we are extending towards the cost-benefit analysis to explicitly evaluate the economic returns to investments in typhoon forecasting and warning infrastructure. This involves collecting budgetary documents from central and local meteorological agencies and estimating the marginal benefits of improved forecast accuracy and earlier warning issuance extrapolating from our nightlight impact regressions. Eventually, we aim to quantify the overall efficiency of the typhoon forecasting and response system in China, and analyze further heterogeneity across provinces with differential policies regarding typhoon alerts.

Our project has underscored the importance of the quality and policy adaptation of extreme weather event forecasting. As climate risks continue to intensify in future years, improving the efficiency of this system will be important for enhancing resilience against natural disasters like typhoons for the populous economic centers of coastal China. This paper contributes to the growing body of research on the economic value of weather and climate forecasts, providing micro-level evidence on how natural disaster forecast information affects economic activities through explicit policy-mediated channels. Our research also contributes to the literature on urban adaptation for climate change, emphasizing the role of information and institutions in shaping adaptive responses to extreme weather events. This paper further connects to research on political economy and public decision-making under information uncertainties, highlighting the influence of local government incentives on the effectiveness of disaster response systems.
